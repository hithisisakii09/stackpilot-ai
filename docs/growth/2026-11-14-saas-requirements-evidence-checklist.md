## 1. Why this topic is distinct

Existing private material already covers:
- broad SaaS total cost of ownership;
- seat versus user billing and unused licences;
- renewal notice and cancellation mechanics;
- uptime, support and SLA questions;
- usage-meter and overage rules; and
- CRM data-export completeness before switching.

This brief addresses the earlier buyer task that precedes those decisions:

**How can a small team turn a vague software wish list into requirements that can be verified against a precise product, plan, region and contract before choosing a vendor?**

A feature list is not proof. A demo can show one configured account, while the buyer needs evidence that the required capability is available to their intended subscription, user role, region and workflow. A weighted score can also hide a critical failure if the team has not agreed on must-have gates first.

The proposed article is therefore a practical requirements-to-evidence method, not another vendor comparison, product ranking, security certification checklist or generic TCO guide.

## 6. Full unpublished article draft

# SaaS Vendor Evaluation Checklist: Turn Requirements Into Verifiable Evidence

A software demo can make a product look like a fit. A feature page can make a capability sound straightforward. Neither proves that the capability is included in the plan your team will buy, available in your region, usable by the right role or covered by the contract you will sign.

Before comparing vendors, turn your team's wish list into requirements that can be checked. Record what evidence would prove each requirement, who must review it and what remains unknown.

This guide is a buyer workflow, not a product ranking, security certification or legal review. It does not assume that one vendor is best for every team.

## 1. Start with the work, not the product name

Write one sentence describing the business outcome. For example:

- “The sales team must preserve linked customer and deal records during a CRM change.”
- “The SEO team must audit the sites it is authorized to manage within a predictable monthly and per-audit limit.”
- “The website team must restore a backup within an agreed recovery window.”

These are example requirement statements, not claims about any vendor.

Name the decision owner and the people who will use, administer, pay for and support the product. A requirement that matters to Finance may be invisible in a product demo; a permission or export requirement may matter to the administrator more than to the daily user.

## 2. Agree on must-haves before the demo

Separate requirements into three groups:

- **Must-have:** a failure blocks the purchase or requires an explicit exception from the accountable owner.
- **Should-have:** important, but a documented workaround or alternative may be acceptable.
- **Nice-to-have:** useful, but not a purchase gate.

Do this before scoring vendors. If the team changes the weights after it has a favorite, the final score can disguise a decision that was already made.

For each must-have, write the consequence of failure. “Single sign-on” is not enough. Does the team require a specific identity provider, a particular user group, a defined plan, or an enforceable contract commitment? If the answer is unknown, keep it open rather than assuming the feature is included.

## 3. Turn each requirement into an observable test

A requirement should be specific enough that two reviewers can agree on what counts as evidence.

| Vague request | More testable requirement | Evidence to request |
|---|---|---|
| “It must be secure.” | “Administrators can enforce the access controls our security owner has specified on the exact plan being quoted.” | Current product documentation, plan/edition confirmation, security documentation and an authorized demonstration. |
| “We need integrations.” | “The product must exchange the agreed fields with our named system, with a documented owner for failures.” | Integration documentation, supported direction, field scope, limits and an authorized test using synthetic data. |
| “We need reliable hosting.” | “The service must meet our defined availability and recovery requirements.” | Contractual SLA, exclusions, status history, backup/restore documentation and a controlled restore exercise. |
| “We need to move our data.” | “The required objects, fields, relationships and files can be exported in a usable format by an authorized role.” | Export documentation, permission details, sample output from a permitted test account and a reconciliation plan. |

A successful demo is evidence only for the scenario demonstrated. Record the configuration, user role, plan and test date. Do not generalize one successful test to every account, edition or region.

## 4. Keep an evidence trail

For every requirement, record:

| Field | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Requirement ID and wording | One observable outcome, not a marketing phrase |
| Priority and owner | Must-have / should-have / nice-to-have; accountable reviewer |
| Product context | Product name, edition, plan, add-on, user role and region |
| Evidence type | Official documentation, contract, vendor response, screenshot or authorized test |
| Source and date | Exact URL or document reference and date checked |
| Test conditions | Account configuration, synthetic input, expected result and actual result |
| Limitation | Exclusions, usage cap, permission, regional or billing caveat |
| Status | PASS, FAIL, NOT TESTED or UNKNOWN |
| Next action | Who must answer or test the unresolved item |

Do not treat every source as equally strong. A public feature page may describe a capability; a plan page may show whether it is included; a contract may define what the vendor is obligated to provide; an authorized test may show what happened in one configuration. Keep those evidence types separate.

A source-intake date is not an approval date. Reopen the original document before publication or purchase, and record when it was checked. If a page has no visible update date, say so rather than inventing one.

## 5. Ask security questions at the product level

Security due diligence should not stop at a company-level badge or questionnaire. CISA's Secure by Demand guidance distinguishes enterprise security from product security and encourages customers to ask software manufacturers questions before, during and after procurement.

Depending on the product and the risk, ask whether the vendor can explain:

- how authentication, roles and administrative access work;
- whether logging and single sign-on are available on the plan being quoted;
- how vulnerabilities are reported and handled;
- what product components or software-bill-of-materials information can be provided;
- how incidents, data deletion, backups and subcontractors are handled; and
- which statements are contractual commitments rather than general marketing descriptions.

NIST SP 1326 (published 2026-07-08) provides a due-diligence framework for ICT suppliers, organized around FOCI, provenance, resilience, foundational cyber practices and supply-chain tiers; it supplements SP 800-161 Rev. 1. NIST SP 1305 remains a broader CSF 2.0 reference for defining and communicating supplier requirements. These are process aids, not certifications, universal legal mandates, or conclusions that a particular SaaS product is secure. Match review depth to the data, access and business impact involved.

## 6. Use a controlled proof of concept

If a requirement cannot be resolved from current documentation, define a small test before starting a trial or proof of concept.

1. Confirm that the organization is authorized to test the product and the data.
2. Use synthetic or non-sensitive data wherever possible.
3. Write the expected result before the test.
4. Record the exact plan, role, region, configuration and date.
5. Save only the minimum evidence needed in an approved location.
6. Record failures and limitations, not just successful screenshots.
7. Ask the vendor to clarify anything that depends on contract terms or an account-specific entitlement.

A proof of concept is not permission to crawl a third-party site, import customer data into an unapproved account or test a vendor's system without authorization.

## 7. Keep price, seats, usage and exit as separate requirements

A requirement checklist should connect to the commercial questions without trying to replace them.

- For the full-term cost, list billing period, seats, setup, migration, add-ons, taxes, variable usage and overages. Use a separate TCO calculation rather than treating a marketing price as the final bill.
- For seats, distinguish people, permissions, purchased capacity and the date a billing change takes effect.
- For renewals, record notice windows, renewal dates, price changes and cancellation method.
- For service reliability, compare the actual SLA measurement, exclusions, support response and recovery commitments.
- For exit, define the data, relationships, files, retention period and deletion evidence required.

These are different buyer jobs. A requirements matrix should link to the evidence for each one instead of repeating one generic “total cost” or “migration” score.

## 8. Make the decision without hiding unknowns

A simple decision rule is more useful than a precise-looking score:

- A failed must-have is a no-go unless the accountable owner approves a documented exception.
- A must-have marked NOT TESTED or UNKNOWN remains open; it is not a pass.
- A should-have can be accepted only with a named owner and a recorded workaround or risk.
- Nice-to-haves can inform a later comparison, but should not outweigh a failed purchase gate.
- Any weighted score should use criteria and weights agreed before vendor demonstrations and should never erase a critical failure.

The result may be “not enough evidence yet.” That is a valid procurement outcome. It is better than choosing a product based on an assumption that no one has verified.

## A reusable requirements record

Copy this structure into your own approved procurement document:

| ID | Requirement | Priority | Owner | Evidence requested | Product / plan / region | Source date | Status | Gap / next action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R-01 | [Observable outcome] | Must / Should / Nice | [Role] | [Document or test] | [Exact context] | [Date] | PASS / FAIL / NOT TESTED / UNKNOWN | [Owner and next step] |

Keep vendor answers and sensitive evidence in your organization's approved systems. Do not upload customer data, contracts or confidential screenshots to a public checklist.

## What this checklist can and cannot do

A requirements worksheet can help a team write down questions and track evidence. It cannot verify a vendor's claims, inspect a private account, certify security, calculate a binding quote, interpret a contract or make a purchase decision for the team.

StackPilot's existing software requirements worksheet is a local question generator for CRM, SEO and hosting. Its selections are generated in the browser and are not submitted to StackPilot; its CSV leaves vendor-answer fields blank and marks them unverified. The page also discloses that hosting-level analytics may record page visits from CDN logs. Confirm the live privacy notice and hosting settings before any public release.

Use the worksheet to prepare questions, then obtain current answers from the provider and the people responsible for your own security, finance and operations decisions.
